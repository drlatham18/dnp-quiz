# S3: Where does the extra sound fit?

## Draft recording script and transcript

## Teaching objectives

- Place S1, S2 and S3 in the cardiac cycle and distinguish the timing alternatives used in this lesson.
- Explain why the older-adult scenario raises concern without treating the recording as a diagnosis.
- Choose a focused assessment for possible lung involvement in the study case and explain what the alternative checks address.

## Spoken dialogue

**New nurse:** I hear two examples, but I do not yet know how to explain the difference. What should I learn before listening?

**Instructor:** Start with timing. S1 is the first familiar sound, near the beginning of ventricular contraction. S2 marks the transition to relaxation and filling. The interval from S1 to S2 is systole; after S2 comes diastole. Those are landmarks, not a diagnosis.

**New nurse:** So when the question says an extra sound just after S2, it is asking about filling rather than ejection?

**Instructor:** Yes. S3 is an extra low-pitched sound in early filling, associated with rapid filling and its slowing. Contrast S4: it occurs with atrial contraction late in filling, before the next S1. A split S2 is different again: its two valve-closure components separate, normally more during inspiration.

**New nurse:** Could I listen to S3 and conclude that someone has heart failure?

**Instructor:** No. An S3 can occur without disease in some younger people. Age, symptoms and other findings change its significance. In our older-adult study case, the new sound accompanies orthopnea, which means breathlessness lying flat, and a rapid weight increase. Together they raise concern about congestion; they do not prove its cause.

**New nurse:** And the weight change in that question is a clue in a scenario, rather than a universal cutoff? What does congestion mean?

**Instructor:** Correct. Congestion refers to problematic fluid accumulation, including in the lungs. For possible lung involvement, examine breathing effort, oxygen saturation and lung sounds. Crackles may occur, but are not specific to heart failure. Normal oxygen saturation does not rule out disease. Use the whole clinical assessment and approved care pathways.

**New nurse:** Why would checking pulse symmetry or blood pressure when standing not be the best answer to that lung question?

**Instructor:** Those checks can be useful, but address circulation or positional symptoms rather than lung involvement as directly. The question asks you to match an assessment to a concern. It is not asking you to diagnose from the recording or select a treatment.

**New nurse:** Now I can listen for an extra sound after S2, then return to the explanation if I cannot place it. Are these patient recordings?

**Instructor:** These are manikin teaching recordings with amplification disclosed beside them. Use a comfortable volume; loudness does not establish severity.
